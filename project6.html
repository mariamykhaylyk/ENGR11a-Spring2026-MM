Robots and Lasers

This project was about building a line-tracking robot and fitting a chassis to it. My partner and I first assembled and soldered the robot kit, then created two different chassis designs: one made for 3D printing and one made for laser cutting. Along the way we worked together in OnShape, tested our designs, and made adjustments to get the fit and functionality right.

Before beginning, I read the instruction manual for the robot kit step by step (D2-5 Intelligent Tracking Car DIY Kit). The printed manual included with the kit was difficult to follow because the text was small and the images were hard to see. Once we found the online version, the process became much easier since the instructions were clearer and included better photos and zoomed-in views showing the correct orientation of parts. One improvement I would suggest is adding images next to the list of components at the beginning so it is easier to confirm all pieces are present.

Then, we started soldering the electronics kit in class. During assembly, one confusing moment occurred when the instructions showed components on the opposite side of the blue plate, making it unclear where to solder. My partner and I collaborated on the build and worked through issues along the way. We divided it so we took turns each step, and each of us did one side of the board. The soldering process was a bit scary for me at first because of the 300 degree C tool, but I think I faked it till I made it. Once the robot was built, we tested if the robot could follow a line of tape on the ground of the classroom, and it did! Then, we took measurements of it using digital calipers to get started on the car robot’s chassis. Below are photos of the soldering and measuring processes.

3D Printing:

My partner and I worked together to create a design plan for a 3D-printed chassis enclosure for the robot. When thinking about the dimensions, we rounded up our measurements of the robot to be slightly simpler in our chassis.

Five key features we needed were: 1. For the wheels to move; 2. For the sensors to see the ground; 3. To see the red LEDs; 4. To be able to change the batteries; 5. To be able to press the power button. My partner and I worked side by side in class. Since we were both fairly new to the process, instead of dividing the work and designing different parts independently, we focused on helping each other learn how to use OnShape effectively. This approach worked well for us.

Once the design was complete, we exported an STL file from OnShape and used Prusa Slicer to prepare it for 3D printing. Below are screenshots of the design in OnShape and the 3D print trajectory preview and the slicer settings. The settings were simple and standard to my previous prints- 0.20mm speed, generic PLA, and 15% infill. The trajectory preview shows the printer building the chassis layer by layer from the bottom surface upward. I oriented the part flat on the build plate to maximize stability and not need supports. The preview showed us that the print was unlikely to go awry.

Below are also photos of the first 3D print iteration and our plan for the second iteration. The result of the first iteration was pretty great! The only fit tweaks we wanted to make was slightly narrow the chassis to close some gaping on the sides, move the cutout to press the button to the back, add led light cutouts, and make the wheel cutouts look more sleek. We also thought we should add more flare to our car! I remembered we have a barbie at home, and thus it became the Pink Barbie Mobile. This time, my partner designed on paper and guided me while I realized the design on OnShape. I feel much more comfortable with OnShape since the last project and officially prefer it to TinkerCad!

Below are photos of the print previews and the result. We decided to split the bottom and top of the car into separate prints then glue to significantly reduce the amount of supports needed. We clicked automatic supports, which looked good in the preview, but ended up being extremely difficult to remove! Because of this, the steering wheel ended up snapping off. If we did it again, we would reduce support density. Nevertheless, I’m very happy with the final outcome as it gives our Barbie a fun ride!

Laser cutting:

My partner and I worked together to plan a laser-cut chassis enclosure for the robot with the same 5 key features as for the 3D print. I measured the robot base again to ensure the box shape would still be a snug fit despite the simpler shape.

My partner and I also worked side by side on this design. We helped each other through different iterations as we refined the model. At first, we tried modifying our original 3d print design, but that ended up being difficult, so we decided it was easier to start with a new sketch. We ultimately followed a video tutorial that demonstrated a simple way to create a laser-cut finger joint box on OnShape ( https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YfDmlx2LpFo ).

Once the design was complete, we imported it into LightBurn. We adjusted the cut settings to ensure the machine would cut through certain pieces while only engraving others. The blue lines indicate full cuts, while the red lines were set as engravings. We set the speed to 60 mm/sec and the power to 80%. Below are the screenshots and the outcome. You can see that the laser cut car was too small to fully fit on the robot because we did not account for the 3mm extrusion inward when making the walls. If we had time for a second iteration, we would account for that space in the dimensions.

Reflection:

This project helped me get more comfortable with the full design process, from assembling electronics to creating and iterating on physical designs. I learned how important accurate measurements and tolerances are when designing something that needs to fit around existing hardware. The 3D printing part went well overall, and through the iterations I became much more confident using OnShape and preparing files in the slicer.

The laser-cut version helped show how designing for laser cutting is different from designing for 3D printing. Our laser chassis ended up slightly too small because we didn’t account for the 3 mm thickness when designing the walls, but that was a helpful lesson about thinking carefully about material dimensions and fit. If we had time for another iteration, we would adjust the dimensions to account for that.

Overall, working with my partner made the process a lot easier because we could troubleshoot problems together and help each other learn the tools. It was also fun to add some creativity to the project, especially when our final 3D-printed design turned into the Pink Barbie Mobile.
