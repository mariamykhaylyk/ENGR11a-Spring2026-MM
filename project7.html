Prosthetic Arm

This project focused on 3D printing and assembling an open-source prosthetic hand, then designing a modification for a specific daily task. My partner and I printed all the parts, followed video and written instructions to assemble the Phoenix Hand, documented tricky steps, tested its functionality, and created a fork attachment to explore task-specific use.

Part 1- Learning to Read the Ecosystem:

There are several public repositories such as e-NABLE Hub, NIH 3D Print Exchange, and the Makers Making Change Catalog that provide free downloadable files for assistive devices. While all three are open to anyone, they differ in focus and usability. From what I saw, e-NABLE is the most specialized, NIH has the widest range, and Makers Making Change focuses on simple, quick prints.

The Makers Making Change Catalog is the simplest of the three. It has around 197 devices, most of which are small and easy to print. Many are one-piece designs, so they do not require detailed instructions beyond printer settings. This can make it very accessible for beginners or non-English speakers. The downside is that the devices are less complex and less functional than something like a prosthetic hand.

The NIH 3D Print Exchange has thousands of health-related models, including over 100 prosthetic designs. However, I found it harder to navigate. Assistive devices are not always clearly grouped, which hurts findability. The quality of documentation varies depending on the design. Some builds are clear, while others are harder to follow. Visuals and troubleshooting are inconsistent. In my opinion, this platform is better suited for users with some prior experience.

The e-NABLE Hub is focused entirely on prosthetics. It is not the easiest to navigate at first, but it offers more depth than the others. One major strength is the community. Users can ask questions and get help, which makes up for gaps in the instructions. This can make e-NABLE more useful for complex builds, even if the documentation itself is not beginner-friendly.

For the Phoenix Hand, I used a written manual and a video tutorial from the V3 Thingiverse page. The written manual ( https://drive.google.com/file/d/1caT-qSH-01H-uLoA75pY2E4LHxce84tP/view ) included photos, but the steps were not labeled. Since many parts, especially the pins, look very similar, this made it confusing to follow. I think the manual had decent visuals but lacked clarity and completeness, so it was not very useful.

The video tutorial ( https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CXoVSTgzyec ) was more helpful because it showed the full process. However, it sometimes moved too quickly through important steps, especially when assembling the fingers. We had to pause and zoom in to understand the orientation of parts. While the video improved clarity, it still was not easy to follow. Neither resource included troubleshooting, which made mistakes harder to fix. Both assume some prior experience in 3D printing, which limits accessibility.

Comparing the same device across platforms, I think e-NABLE is more useful because of its community support. Even if the documentation is not perfect, you can get help. NIH is less consistent and harder to navigate, so I would be less likely to rely on it for a complex build.

If I were designing my own repository, I would focus on organization and usability. I would sort devices by type and add filters for difficulty, print time, and materials. Each design would require a clear step-by-step manual, a video tutorial, and a troubleshooting section. I would also include tags like beginner-friendly or language options.

Overall, all three repositories are useful but have different strengths. e-NABLE is best for prosthetics and support, NIH offers variety but lacks consistency, and Makers Making Change is most accessible for simple builds. In my opinion, the biggest issue across all three is the lack of clear, complete, and beginner-friendly documentation for more complex devices.

Part 2- 3D Print & Assemble the Prosthesis:

We found the Phoenix Hand V2 and V3 models on Thingiverse and printed all required parts in PLA at 150% scale using Prusa Mini+ printers and PrusaSlicer. We used the Thingiverse Phoenix Hand V2 right palm and handle ( https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:1453190 ), with V3 fingers, arm guard, and all other parts ( https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:4056253#google_vignette ). We scaled these all by 150% to fit.

Our assembly process and obstacles:

1. Our first palm print failed and produced weak, crackable material. This was probably due to the specific shiny material we used and/or printer issues, so we reprinted it in a different work station. We decided to use a pre-made palm from the extra parts drawer for this class period until our new print was ready, although it had a grate at the bottom instead of a bar as it was made to mold to someone who actually needed the prosthetic hand.

2. We first tried using the V3 written manual ( https://drive.google.com/file/d/1caT-qSH-01H-uLoA75pY2E4LHxce84tP/view ), but it lacked labeled steps. Many parts, especially pins, look similar but are slightly different, which made the manual difficult to follow. Because of this, we switched to the video tutorial.

3. The video was more helpful ( https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CXoVSTgzyec ), but some steps were too fast, especially during finger assembly. We had to pause and zoom in to understand part orientation. At one point, we assembled the thumb incorrectly and had to redo it.

4. Once the fingers were done, our new palm still wasn’t ready, so we tried to heat the grate at the bottom of the palm to make it fit my hand better. Although this worked to an extent, we definitely wanted to have something to grip onto, so we waited until our new palm print was finished to assemble the rest.

5. We assembled the hand and added rubber bands. However, several pegs from the fingers popped off, making it impossible to rubber band. This is probably due to low infill (15%) making the parts too weak, and potentially the type of shiny PLA we used. We replaced the broken pieces with spare parts.

6. We were ready to attach the arm guard. We heated it and formed it around a cylindrical shape. We had to be especially particular in forming the part of the arm guard that attaches to the palm to make sure they align. We then stuck on padding for comfort.

7. We then started working on adding the tensioned strings to actually make the fingers move based on how you hold your wrist. First, we assembled the tensioning mechanism and clicked it into the arm guard. But, we encountered an obstacle– the tensioner pins were too thick for the tensioner block because the raft layer on the pins would not come off. Therefore, we sanded the sides down and got to use a belt sander for the first time. Then, we strung the arm by following the video tutorial closely. This also prompted us to do some problem solving– the strings were incredibly difficult to maneuver through some of the tunnels of the palm. We solved this by using a wire as a long makeshift needle, and we were able to thread the string through in seconds!

Functional analysis: The hand grips using the wrist mechanism, but not the most evenly across all fingers, but that can be adjusted with the strings and tension box. Hard to pick up a water bottle or something small. The hand works better for large and light objects but struggles with smaller or more precise items. Overall, it demonstrates the basic mechanics of a wrist-driven prosthetic, but has limitations in strength, precision, and durability.

Part 4- Design a Task-Specific Modification:

For this part, we designed a modification that allows the Phoenix hand to assist with eating using a fork. We created a 3D printed fork attachment that screws directly into the palm of the hand.

Functional Requirements: Eating with a fork requires a stable grip, the ability to apply downward force, and enough control to guide food to the mouth. The attachment needs to stay securely in place, resist rotation, and move easily. It also needs to be easy to attach and remove.

Design Approach: Our design snaps a screw base into the palm, allowing a fork to be screwed in when needed. This makes the design more versatile, since the same connection system could be used for other tools, not just a fork.

SIT Pattern: Task Unification: We applied the Task Unification pattern by giving the palm an additional function. Instead of only acting as a base for the hand, it also serves as a mounting point for tools. This reduces the need for separate gripping mechanisms and simplifies the design.

Modeling and Fabrication: We first sketched the idea on paper, then modeled the attachment in OnShape. After exporting and slicing the file, we printed the fork using a 3D printer and tested the fit with the Phoenix Hand. The attachment snapped right into place securely and did not require glue or additional support!

Testing and Functional Analysis: The fork attachment worked as intended. It was easy to attach and remained stable during use. However, the plastic material is not food safe, so for real use, the attachment would need to be printed with food-safe filament or combined with a metal fork.

Overall, the modification successfully enables the task and demonstrates how the e-NABLE hand can be adapted for specific daily activities.

Part 5- Reflect, Contribute Back, & Bridge to the Capstone:

Ethical Reflection: This project really showed both the potential and limits of open-source assistive tech. One idea from the Microsoft Inclusive Design Toolkit is thinking about who gets left out. Open-source designs are free and widely available, but if someone doesn’t have a 3D printer, the right materials, or the know-how, they can’t actually use them. That’s a gap in accessibility. Sara Hendren talks about designing with users, not just for them. Some of the Phoenix Hand designs work, but they weren’t always easy or comfortable without trial and error. The volunteer-driven model is great for innovation, but it also means documentation and quality vary a lot. Things aren’t as reliable as traditional medical devices, which is worth keeping in mind.

Contribution Back: We made a FAQ “lessons learned” document based on our build, including print settings, common failures, and fixes. Key takeaways: increase infill to make parts stronger, skip rafts on parts that don’t need them to reduce hassle, sand down pins if raft material sticks, use wire to thread strings through the palm. I think this kind of guide is more useful than a basic manual because it highlights the real pain points we ran into and how to troubleshoot, which can save others time and frustration.

Bridge to the Capstone: A big challenge we noticed is usability. Small mistakes during assembly can really affect how the hand works, and some steps are just not intuitive. For the capstone, I’d want our team to focus on making prosthetic designs more user-friendly and modular. That could mean better attachment systems and/or clearer instructionss. Making it simpler and more intuitive would make these devices usable for a lot more people, not just those with technical experience.
