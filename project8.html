Snazzy Shoez: Concept

For my seventh and final project, I worked with Rebecca Dranikoff and Sam Pizarro to solve this problem statement: Individuals with only one functional hand lack a practical way to tie standard shoelaces because existing solutions fail to provide full autonomy and are too conspicuous for formal wear. This is part 1 of our project, the brainstorming stage.

~Research & Problem Framing~

The first step to our project was to create a Group Contract. You can view that here.

Problem statement: Individuals with only one functional hand lack a practical way to tie standard shoelaces because existing solutions fail to provide full autonomy and are too conspicuous for formal wear.

The target customer we are catering to is someone with one functioning hand. We want something both a professional can wear to work and a kid could wear to school with swagger. There are 3 million people worldwide who have an arm amputation, which makes up 30% of all amputees, with 2.4 million of arm amputees living in developing countries (OSU.edu). This highlights the need for something accessible and cheap. According to Hospital News, “In North America, there are over 58 million people of all ages experiencing limited hand mobility, hand and arm weakness and difficulty grasping and controlling objects, because of a medical condition or injury.”

We contacted an Occupational Therapist, Meaghan Myers, who said there are 3 main difficult steps for tying your shoes with one hand: crossing to make an x, making the bunny ear for the loop, and pulling the string through the loop. This was helpful, but we were hoping to understand the process better and with more detail. We have not yet spoken to someone with first hand experience, but we plan to later on in the process.

We researched the existing solutions and 3D printed some open source designs to better understand their strengths and weaknesses. We also simply tried to tie our shoes one-handed and then used youtube tutorials. The 3 open source designs we printed were a tensioner block, a shoelace clip, and a shoelace guide.

~Tension Block~

To make the tension block stronger, we used PETG filament and changed the infill to gyroid. The cam (the teardrop shaped part) rotates so you can clamp one lace underneath it to create tension. You keep one foot on the flat bottom throughout the process. The peg on the top is used for creating one of the loops.

STRENGTHS: the teardrop shape was effective in pulling the strings taut and having a small peg for the loop to go around was helpful.

WEAKNESSES: bulky and not easily portable, requires very long laces.

~Shoelace Clip~

The other two prints were printed in PLA. The shoelace clip has three parts that fit into each other. You thread the laces through the 3 holes on either side and tuck the remaining length of the laces into the shoe. This device stays on your shoe permanently. To get your shoe on and off, you press on one side which releases the other to loosen the laces.

STRENGTHS: easy to use one-handed once set-up, avoids having to retie your shoes every day.

WEAKNESSES: large and conspicuous, uncomfortable with the extra laces tucked in.

~Shoelace Guide~

The shoelace guide was successful in our test. You thread both laces through the two parallel holes, and then thread one lace through the third hole to make a loop. You then make a loop with the other lace and tie the laces together. To untie your shoelaces, you pull the lace out of the third hole and unpick the knot.

STRENGTHS: discreet, inconspicuous, portable, allows traditional shoelace knots.

WEAKNESSES: time-consuming to use, easy to lose.

~Problem Definiton & Brain Storming~

In 3 minutes, each member listed as many aspects of the shoe tying process as we could think of. This was helpful to get the ball rolling in terms of brainstorming and collaboration. We already started to see some common ideas, such as the importance of gripping, tension, and efficiency.

~Next, we started our structured “Thematic Analysis”: writing down words, phrases, quotes, etc. that stood out to us from research. We answered the guiding questions below.~

○ Take a good long look across your interviews and other research. Have any patterns emerged? Is there a compelling insight you heard again and again? --> The main challenges are maneuvering and looping the string into a knot and the lack of a second point of control to manage tension.

○ A consistent problem the people you’re designing for face? --> Devices, modified laces, or alternative shoes focus on replacing tying altogether rather than supporting the actual process. People should be able to choose any shoe they want.

○ What feels significant? --> Autonomy, efficiency, and pride.

○ What surprised you? --> Our interviewee mentioned one of the hardest things to do would be crossing to make an x, which we did not consider to be a problem.

Repeating themes: autonomy, freedom of choice, and efficiency.

~Based on our thematic analysis, we came up with 3 insight statements.~

1. A person without a working hand feels burdensome when someone else needs to tie their shoes for them.

2. A person without a working hand feels limited when they go shoe shopping.

3. A person without a working hand desires efficiency when getting ready in the morning.

~We then further developed those into 3 “How Might We” Statements.~

1. How might we provide someone with additional autonomy in their daily lives?

2. How might we open up the opportunity for disabled individuals to purchase typical, non-modified items at a store?

3. How might we speed up the process of getting ready in the morning for people with limited dexterity?

~We brainstormed ideas under our three “How Might We” statements.~

We put down any and all ideas, even the ones that were obscure or infeasible. Then, we came up with 3 top ideas based on the overlapping thoughts from the three sections. The goal was to choose ideas that were portable, practical, work with any laced shoe, and quick. Our top ideas were:

1. Hook on side of shoe: discrete, portable, minimalist, cheap.

2. Clamp: basic, mobile, practical, creates tension.

3. Guiding device: stays on shoe, not as novel, good for teaching.

The idea we decided to work on was a modification of the “hook on side of shoe” thought. Rather than a hook, we realized we could use a simple post. You can stick the post through one of the top holes of your shoe, and use it to wrap the loop around. It could be easily portable with a keychain or it can remain in your shoe throughout the day. We also realized that kids could put a small design on the end of it, sort of like jibbitz for Crocs, to customize it. The “snazzy shoez” were born!

~Storyboard~

We created a storyboard to show the steps needed to successfully tie your shoe with our final selected idea, the Snazzy Shoez. We do not currently have any printed design, but we used an M3*20 screw as a prototype; it worked really well and is pictured in the storyboard.
